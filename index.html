The big picture: the world sold, India did not

3 shocks hit world markets at once, and India was the odd one out.

In short
  • US prices rose faster than expected, so the cost of borrowing went up again.
  • Lenders began to doubt the firms that build AI data centres, and chip shares in Korea crashed.
  • India's shares still rose, helped by talk of lower share taxes and a large bank sale.

Why India held up

On 13 October the Finance Minister said she was open to a debate on long-term capital gains tax and the securities transaction tax (a small tax on every share trade). Shares jumped. Then the cabinet cleared the sale of IDBI Bank to Fairfax, which showed foreign money still wants Indian banks (section 05).

One view is that India now moves against the world: it lagged when AI shares soared, so it falls less when they crash, and money may rotate in. The opposite view is that India has simply been left out. Foreign funds are still selling, and India's own problems, from inflation to the rupee, have not gone away.

The thread that ties it together

Reading between the lines, the AI boom and India's pain are linked. AI firms borrow huge sums, which adds to the demand for money and helps push US rates up. High US rates pull money out of places like India and weaken the rupee. So if the AI borrowing spree slows, US rates could ease and India could gain. That is the hopeful reading.

The worried reading is that a sudden end to AI lending could look like 2008, when bad loans spread from one corner of the market to banks everywhere. US bank shares are already weak (section 02). Which reading wins depends on whether the stress stays inside a few AI lenders or spreads.

02

The price of money: US inflation came back

Fuel pushed US prices up again, and borrowing costs, banks and gold all felt it.

In short
  • US prices rose 3.7% in a year, more than expected, mostly because of fuel.
  • Traders now think the US central bank may raise rates again soon.
  • Bank shares and gold fell, while China kept buying gold at a record pace.

Fuel drove the surprise

US consumer prices rose 0.6% in September alone, the biggest monthly rise in 5 months. Core inflation (prices without food and fuel) was 2.6% for the year, just above forecasts. The chart shows where the heat sits: almost all of it is in energy and travel.

The Fed (the US central bank) already raised rates in September, its first rise since July 2023. Reading between the lines, a central bank that raises rates while fuel is the cause is trying to stop prices spreading into wages. Workers' pay rose about 3%, so prices are now eating all of the gain, which makes the Fed's job harder.

Borrowing costs at 20-year highs

The US 10-year yield (what lenders demand to lend to the US government for that long) jumped above 5.3% after the data, then fell back. A client survey by Goldman Sachs says this level is the highest since 2002.

  • US 5-year — highest since 2007; the 2-year ended near 4.79% and the 30-year near 5.52%.
  • Dollar — a broad dollar index hit its highest since November, and traders bet on 3 more Fed rises by the end of 2027.
  • US Treasury — offered to buy back up to $6bn of its own long bonds to calm the market.
  • Europe — economist Robin Brooks notes long-dated borrowing costs in France and Italy rose twice as fast as in the US in a day; he calls them the weakest links in the euro area.
  • Japan — banks and insurers are selling foreign bonds and buying home bonds, and a Bank of Japan board member says the neutral rate is well above 1%.
  • Britain — Bank of England economist Huw Pill says a full 1% rise may not be needed.

US banks are in a bear market

Bank of America fell 8.3%, its worst day since April 2025, after a $1.2bn fine over money-laundering and sanctions failures and a profit miss. It is now among the largest US banks that are more than a fifth below their peak (a bear market).

One view is that US families owe about $10 trillion in loans, so even a modest rise in bad loans would eat a big part of bank capital. The opposite view comes from the banks themselves: card spending is still rising fast at Citi and JPMorgan, and lower-income customers look stable. US banks also carry unrealised losses on bonds of $326.7bn, a slow drag rather than a sudden one.

So the problem is less about bad loans today and more about trading and rates. Trading income at Bank of America fell 29% from the quarter before, and JPMorgan cut its share buyback to about $1bn from $6.7bn. Morgan Stanley and BlackRock, which earn from fees and markets, did well (section 09 has the AI angle).

Gold fell, but central banks did not stop buying

Gold slipped to about $4,100 an ounce, and silver and gold mining shares fell harder. Higher US rates hurt metals because they pay no interest. Yet the biggest buyer kept going: China's central bank bought 23 tonnes in September, its 23rd month of buying in a row.

  • Russia — will buy about $3.3bn of gold and foreign money in a month, about 5 times the month before, from oil income above $59 a barrel.
  • Producers — Laos is refining its own gold, Indonesia taxes gold exports at up to 15%, and China limits exports, so less metal reaches the market.
  • India — silver on the MCX exchange is back to ₹2,07,990 a kg, its March low.

One view is that gold is in a long bull market because governments want it in place of US dollar savings. The other view is that markets sell gold whenever rates rise, and rates are rising. Both can be true: central banks buy slowly over years, while traders sell fast on the day.

03

Oil, ships and war: the sea lanes got more dangerous

Attacks on ships rose to a record pace, and diesel stayed the tightest fuel of all.

In short
  • Iran and its allies attacked more ships near the Strait of Hormuz than ever before.
  • Experts disagree on whether the world has too much oil or too little.
  • Diesel is scarce, and Indian refiners are earning record margins from it.

A record pace of attacks at sea

The Strait of Hormuz is the narrow sea passage out of the Gulf. Before the war that began with US and Israeli strikes on Iran in late February, about 1 in 5 barrels of the world's oil and gas passed through it. Now ships are being hit at a record rate, and the attacks have spread to the Gulf of Oman and the waters off Qatar and the UAE.

  • Spotters — Iran's Revolutionary Guards use unarmed men in small boats to find targets; US forces destroyed 1 such boat in the Gulf of Oman.
  • Saudi Arabia — Houthi fighters from Yemen attacked the headquarters of Saudi Aramco at Dhahran and say strikes on Saudi airports and oil sites will go on.
  • Iran — a senior Iranian official, Mohsen Rezaei, said Iran targets 5 or 6 ships every night.
  • United States — keeps a blockade on Iranian ports; reports say President Trump will not attack again before the US mid-term vote.

Reading between the lines, Iran does not need to close the strait to win. Keeping insurers and crews afraid is enough to keep oil dear, and dear fuel in the US hurts the party in power before a vote. That is why the attacks rose just as US inflation became the top story (section 02).

Too much oil, or too little?

Brent crude, the main world oil price, spiked to $107 a barrel before easing to about $104. The IEA (the International Energy Agency, which tracks oil for rich countries) says world output is back above 100 million barrels a day and forecasts a surplus next year.

The East-West pipeline was shut by a drone attack on 11 September; the expansion is planned over 2 to 3 years.

One view, from the energy research firm HFI Research, is that the IEA is wrong because its forecast assumes Hormuz is back to normal, and that supply growth outside OPEC is ending. The opposite view, from Javier Blas at Bloomberg, uses the shipping data: crude flows from the Middle East, without Iran, are back to about 75% of pre-war levels, and early October was better than September.

  • Gulf premium — the extra price for Gulf crude (Oman over Dubai) was near $63 in March, about $33 in September and only about $7 to $8 now.
  • Kuwait — still struggling to ship; the UAE, Iraq and Saudi Arabia are managing.
  • Oil against gold — a barrel of oil buys less gold than at almost any time since 1987, though the ratio has started to rise.

So the price of crude is being held up by fear more than by missing barrels. If the attacks stop, the price could fall fast. If a large tanker or a Saudi terminal is hit, it could jump just as fast.

Diesel is the real shortage

The IEA says diesel will stay tight "well into 2027". Government oil stocks in rich countries are the lowest since at least December 1989. US diesel futures have roughly doubled over the period charted, to about $4.94 a gallon.

Ukrainian drone strikes on Russian refineries, verified, per month in 2026

Count of verified strikes shared in the material (92 from January to 28 September).

The chart shows why. Ukraine keeps hitting Russian refineries with drones, and Russia's diesel exports fell from about 817,000 barrels a day to about 150,000 by mid-September. Russia banned diesel exports in July. Ukraine has now said it will resume the strikes after a short pause.

  • Deal — President Trump announced a deal for Russia to send 300,000 tonnes of diesel, and pushed the G7 to release 100 million barrels of oil.
  • Ukraine — President Zelensky says he feels betrayed by the diesel deal.
  • India — for India, which faced 14 months of US threats over buying Russian oil, the US buying Russian diesel looks awkward.
  • Coal — Asian coal prices are at a 2-year high, up more than 40% this year, as buyers switch fuels.

Views split on the deal. One view is that it adds real diesel to a short market. The opposite view is that it only relabels Russian fuel that was already being sold around sanctions, so no new supply reaches buyers.

India's refiners win, oil sellers lose

Wide diesel margins are a gift to refiners. MRPL (Mangalore Refinery and Petrochemicals) made a quarterly profit of about ₹1,900 crore, about 3 times a year earlier, even though it processed less crude. Its operating margin roughly tripled in a single quarter.

One view is that this is a new normal while the war lasts. The opposite view is that it is a price and stock gain that reverses when the war ends. Earlier in the war, HPCL lost ₹11,526 crore in a single quarter when it could not raise pump prices, which shows how fast the cycle can turn.

US Secretary of State Marco Rubio will visit Mumbai and Jamnagar, home to the world's largest refinery, but not Delhi. Reading between the lines, energy is now the main business between the 2 countries: India wants crude and gas, and the US wants buyers for its fuel.

04

India's share market: local savers against foreign sellers

Talk of lower share taxes lifted prices, foreign funds kept selling, and a long line of share sales is forming.

In short
  • Shares had their best day in 4 months when the Finance Minister spoke about share taxes.
  • Foreign funds still sold on most days, while Indian savers poured money into smaller companies.
  • India's largest ever share sale, by Jio Platforms, opens soon.

A tax hint moved the market

Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman said she is keenly hearing the case on long-term capital gains tax (12.5% on profits from shares held over a year) and the securities transaction tax. The Sensex rose 1,109 points to 73,033 on 13 October. Nilesh Shah of Kotak Mahindra AMC had raised the issue with her.

One view is that India needs foreign money badly, so it should drop capital gains tax for foreign investors and tax people where they live, as many countries do. The opposite view is that tax is not the main reason foreigners left: once oil falls below $100, flows may turn on their own, as analysts at Motilal Oswal argue.

Foreign funds keep selling

Foreign funds sold Indian shares on all but 1 day in October. They took out ₹1.64 lakh crore in the first half of the financial year, and have sold a net $62.4bn since the market peak.

India's economy and banks: fast growth, rising prices

Inflation spread, the RBI fought for the rupee, and the government sold a bank at a good price.

In short
  • Prices in India are rising faster, and wholesale prices rose the most in 4 years.
  • The RBI used new rules to pull the rupee back from record lows.
  • The sale of IDBI Bank to Fairfax shows foreign buyers still want Indian banks.

Inflation has spread beyond food

Retail inflation rose to 5.54% in September, about a 2-year high. Wholesale inflation, which tracks prices between businesses, jumped to 11.39%, well above forecasts. Fuel and power led, but factory goods also got dearer.

The RBI raised its repo rate (the rate at which it lends to banks) to 5.50% in October, its first rise since February 2023. With retail inflation now at about the same level, the real interest rate is close to zero. One view is that the RBI must raise rates more to end this. The opposite view is that a rate rise when growth depends on borrowing could start a slowdown, and that it is better to watch how Korea and other Asian markets handle the same pressure.

The fight for the rupee

The rupee recovered to 94.99 to the dollar from 96.7 in 2 days after the RBI changed the rules on currency hedges. The aim was to stop traders betting against the rupee. The RBI governor, Sanjay Malhotra, said more steps are possible.

  • Hedges — companies can no longer cancel a currency hedge and book it again at a better rate, and banks must check the real trade behind any hedge above $5m (from $100m).
  • Oil companies — now buy dollars straight from the RBI, so their demand does not hit the market.
  • Cost of hedging — the 3-month forward premium (the yearly cost of locking in a rate) is about 5.3%, the highest since May 2021.
  • Bonds — the 10-year government bond yield hit 7.40%, the highest since March 2023 and up 0.72% since the war began; the 2-year touched 7%.

Reading between the lines, the headline reserve figure looks large, but the RBI has already promised a big part of it in forward deals. That is why it chose rules over spending dollars. If rules stop working, the remaining tools are a sharp rate rise or a cut in how much money Indians may send abroad, as in 2013.

Views split on the rupee itself. One view, voiced by banker Uday Kotak, is that the RBI should let the rupee find its own level. The opposite view, from a macro investor who advises foreign clients, is that a weaker rupee is good for India because it makes Indian factories cheaper for the world.

Chip tools and new factories

China exports its way through

China's exports rose 36.3% in dollar terms in September, far above forecasts, for a record monthly trade surplus. Exports to the US rose 39.6%, and factory-gate prices rose at the fastest pace in about 4 years.

Reading between the lines, China is now exporting price rises, not cheap goods. That adds to the inflation problem in the US (section 02) and to cost pressure on Indian makers who buy Chinese parts. Even so, China's solar giants cut 40,000 more jobs last year despite booming exports, which shows how thin their profits are.

Indian IT: AI cuts both ways

Accenture guided for only 3% to 6% growth next year, though its AI-led deals have multiplied. Salil Parekh of Infosys said AI services are growing by double digits each quarter and could soon be a large share of work.

One view is that Indian IT shares do better when AI shares fall, because money moves from the AI winners back to cheaper, steady firms. The opposite view, from Kotak, reads Accenture's weak guide as a sign that Indian IT will grow slowly for years.

  • Zoho — founder Sridhar Vembu said there will be no pay rises this year because AI costs have surged, and called US markets a "monster bubble".
  • Pay — Aon expects Indian firms overall to raise pay by 9.1% next year, with staff leaving at 18% a year.
  • People — about 12,700 tech workers moved back from the US to India this year to September, while about 14,500 went the other way.
